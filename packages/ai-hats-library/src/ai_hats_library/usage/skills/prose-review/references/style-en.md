# Style reference — English

Plain language (Federal Plain Language Guidelines, plainlanguage.gov): the
reader finds what they need, understands it on the first read, and can act on
it. Plain does not mean simplified — the technical term stays when it is the
right word. Every item below is a rule the critic quotes a line against; no
quote, no finding.

## Syntax

- **One idea per sentence.** Past ~25 words, or two ideas joined by a comma —
  split.
- **Verbs, not nominalizations.** "perform a validation of" → "validate";
  "make a decision" → "decide".
- **Actor in the sentence.** Passive and agentless forms ("it was decided",
  "is considered") — who? Name the actor.
- **Front-load the point.** The sentence's subject and verb come first; the
  qualifying clause follows.
- **Noun stacks.** Three or more nouns in a row ("agent session state
  directory layout") — unpack.

## Words

- **Concrete over abstract.** "Use the tool" → name the tool; "in a timely
  manner" → the deadline.
- **Intensifiers and evaluations without a fact.** "very", "really",
  "extremely", "robust", "efficient", "powerful", "seamless" — a fact, or
  cut.
- **Filler openers.** "It is important to note that", "It should be
  mentioned", "In order to" — cut, keep the statement.
- **Modality without a subject.** "one can", "it is necessary to" — who?
  Name them or make it an instruction.
- **Terms.** One name per thing across the whole text; the glossary's name if
  there is a glossary.

## Tells of machine-written prose

From the Vale package that flags AI-written prose and from reading model
output. One hit is nothing; five on a page mean the text has no author.

- Em-dash as the all-purpose connector, several per paragraph, where a
  period, comma or conjunction was due.
- Triads of abstract nouns: "clarity, precision, and structure".
- "It's not X, it's Y" / "not just X — it's Y" as a device.
- An epigram closing the paragraph that just made the point.
- Symmetric bullet lists of equal length and identical syntax where a person
  would write a paragraph.
- A conclusion that restates the introduction.
- A rhetorical question opening a section ("Why does this matter?").
- "In today's fast-paced world", "let's dive in", "delve", "crucial",
  "leverage", "comprehensive", "landscape", "navigate", "at its core".
- Three parenthetical examples after every noun.
- Hedges in a stack: "generally, in most cases, it is likely that".
